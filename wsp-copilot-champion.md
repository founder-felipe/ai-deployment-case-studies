# Copilot Champion at WSP — AI adoption inside a large engineering consultancy

**What it is:** the enablement half of my work. Not a system I built — a role I took on
inside a global engineering and professional-services firm, running internal AI training and
automating my own reporting work.

**Why it is in a portfolio of deployments:** most failed AI rollouts I have watched failed
on adoption, not capability. The tool worked; nobody changed how they worked. Being the
person who had to actually move a room of engineers, administrators and managers from "I
tried it once and it was wrong" to "I use it in my work" is the part of AI deployment that does
not show up in a repository.

**Scope of this page.** Everything here is at the level of statements that appear on my CV.
There are no slides, no notes, no recordings, no internal learning-system content, no
project names, no client names, and no internal metrics beyond the attendance and my own
before/after timing. That boundary is deliberate and permanent.

---

## What happened

I joined in a business-support role and became the team's default AI resource within weeks,
which turned into the internal Copilot Champion role and a standing fortnightly training
cadence on prompt engineering, workflow automation and responsible use.

The substantial piece was a hybrid prompt-engineering and Microsoft 365 integration workshop
delivered to **170+ professionals at the Perth office in March 2026 — 146 logged online plus
an in-person room**, spanning engineering, administration and management
`[WSP-1: measured (self-reported)]`. It was one of the first structured internal AI education sessions at that
office.

It was then **scaled at the national manager's request into a national rollout, delivered to
~50 attendees across the Australian business-support function in April 2026**, with two
overseas offices scheduled to follow `[WSP-2: measured (self-reported)]`. The recording was
published internally so people outside the live cohort could work through it async.

Separately, I **cut a weekly reporting task from 2.5 hours to 7 minutes — a 95% reduction —
with a custom Copilot automation**, at the same output quality, and wrote it up as a
reusable playbook for the team `[WSP-3: measured (self-reported)]`.

## How honest that 95% is

It is my own measurement of my own task. Before: a recurring, largely mechanical weekly
report I had timed by doing it many times. After: the automated path, timed the same way.
No third party audited either number, and I did not run a controlled comparison.

What that number *does* support: a repeated, mechanical, well-specified task with a stable
input format is a good automation target, and the saving is large enough that the
measurement error does not change the conclusion.

What it *does not* support, and I will not imply: that the same reduction transfers to
anyone else's task, that the org saw a 95% productivity gain anywhere, or that adoption of
the playbook was measured at all `[WSP-4: unknown — never claimed]`. I do not have adoption
data. Attendance is not adoption, and I am not going to present it as if it were.

## What actually moved people

Four things worked, and they are the ones I would repeat anywhere.

**Teach the failure mode before the capability.** The room's prior was "I tried it, it made
something up, I stopped." Leading with where the tool is wrong, and why, buys more
credibility in ten minutes than a demo reel buys in an hour — and it is the honest framing
anyway.

**Use their work, not a toy.** Generic prompt exercises teach prompt exercises. Live rework
of a document the room actually produces every week teaches the thing.

**Ship one artifact people can steal.** A single documented, reusable playbook for a task
everybody does beats a comprehensive framework nobody opens. The reporting automation became
that artifact because it was mine, boring, and immediately copyable.

**Stay inside the sanctioned tool.** The organisation had one approved assistant. Teaching
that one deeply — rather than the model I would have picked personally — is what made the
training usable rather than a compliance problem. Enablement that requires people to break
policy is not enablement.

## Why this sits next to the engineering pages

The technical case studies in this repository are about keeping a deployed system honest.
This one is about the other half of the same job: whether anyone changes their behaviour
because of it. A forward-deployed engineer who can build the pipeline but cannot get the
room to use it has shipped nothing.

It is also the reason the claims discipline in this repository exists at all. Standing in
front of 170 colleagues and being asked "does it actually work" teaches you very quickly
which of your numbers you can defend and which you have been repeating.
